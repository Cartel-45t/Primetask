Transcription is more than typing what you hear.

01 · Foundations

What is transcription?

Transcription is the process of converting spoken content from audio or video into written text. The transcriber listens to the source, identifies the words and relevant sounds, organizes the material according to the requested style, and checks the finished document against the recording.

Core rule: the recording is the source of truth. Do not add information because it sounds sensible, remove information because it seems repetitive, or silently repair a speaker's claim. The job is to represent what was said according to the agreed transcription style.
A

Accuracy

Capture the actual words, names, figures, technical terms, and relationships between ideas. Replay unclear sections instead of guessing.

B

Consistency

Apply one set of rules throughout the document. Speaker labels, timestamps, punctuation, numbers, and terminology should follow the same pattern.

C

Confidentiality

Handle recordings and transcripts only through authorized channels. Do not forward, publish, or reuse client material without permission.

02 · Transcription styles

Know the type before you begin

Different projects require different levels of detail. Always follow the client's instructions or the task brief. If the requested style is unclear, obtain clarification before making choices that could change the transcript.

Full detail

1. Strict / True Verbatim

Strict verbatim records speech as closely as practical to how it occurs. It can include repeated words, filler sounds, false starts, stutters, grammatical slips, and meaningful non-speech vocalizations.

Include: “I, I think we, uh, we should start again,” if those words and sounds are audible and relevant to the required standard.

Do not: silently remove repetitions, polish grammar, replace informal wording with formal wording, or make a hesitant speaker sound fluent.

Often used for: linguistic research, detailed interviews, some legal or investigative work, and analysis where the manner of speaking matters.

Readable fidelity

2. Clean Verbatim

Clean verbatim preserves the speaker's meaning and wording while removing non-essential speech clutter that does not affect meaning. Depending on the brief, this may include habitual fillers, accidental repeated words, and abandoned starts.

Include: the complete intended message, natural phrasing, meaningful emphasis, and relevant corrections made by the speaker.

Do not: rewrite sentences extensively, remove meaningful hesitation, change the speaker's position, or summarize a long explanation into a short statement.

Example: “I, uh, wanted to, wanted to ask about the report” may become “I wanted to ask about the report” when the filler and repetition add no meaning.

Edited for meaning

3. Intelligent / Edited Transcription

Intelligent transcription creates a clear, readable version of spoken content while preserving its intended meaning. It may correct obvious speech disfluencies and organize awkward spoken phrasing, within the limits of the assignment.

Include: accurate claims, complete key information, logical paragraphs, and standard written punctuation.

Do not: introduce facts, improve the speaker's argument with your own ideas, change technical meaning, or remove important qualifications. Never turn a transcript into a summary unless asked.

Often used for: business discussions, general interviews, educational material, and content intended for easy reading.

Time-linked

4. Timestamped Transcription

Timestamps mark the point in the recording where a word, phrase, sentence, speaker turn, or segment begins. The required interval depends on the task instructions.

Common formats: [00:01:12] for a point in time, or [00:01:12 --> 00:01:28] for a segment with start and end times.

Include: timecodes that match the recording, consistent formatting, and the requested timestamp frequency.

Do not: estimate timecodes from memory, invent an end time, use inconsistent formats, or place timestamps so inaccurately that the passage cannot be located.

Speaker identification

5. Speaker-Labeled Transcription

Each speaker turn is identified with a consistent label. Use the supplied names if authorized and available. Otherwise, use neutral labels such as Male_1:, Female_1:, or Speaker_1:, according to the project convention.

Include: a label whenever the speaker changes, stable numbering throughout, and separate turns when speakers alternate.

Do not: guess a person's identity, change labels midway, combine two people into one speaker, or infer gender or identity from unreliable evidence. If the voice cannot be identified confidently, use the approved uncertain-speaker label.

Combined format

6. Timestamped + Speaker-Labeled

This format combines speaker identification with time-linked segments. It is useful when reviewers need to know both who said a passage and where it occurs in the recording.

Example:
Male_1:
[00:00:12 --> 00:00:25]
Today, we will review the main findings.

Include: the speaker label, timestamp line, and corresponding spoken content in the requested order.

Do not: put the timecode against the wrong passage, omit a speaker change, or let the timestamp convention drift from section to section.

Exact text

7. Strict / Full Verbatim with Timestamps

This combines detailed verbatim capture with timecodes. Fillers, repetitions, false starts, and relevant vocalizations are retained while timestamps anchor the speech to the source.

Include: the detail required by the verbatim standard and accurate timecodes at the required points.

Do not: clean up disfluencies just to make the document look polished, or sacrifice timecode accuracy for speed.

Edited text

8. Clean Verbatim with Speaker Labels

This is clean verbatim presented as separate, clearly labeled speaker turns. It keeps the exchange easy to follow without preserving every non-essential filler.

Include: consistent labels and the full meaningful contribution of each speaker.

Do not: merge separate speakers into one paragraph, attribute a statement to the wrong person, or edit the content into a summary.

Specialized

9. Intelligent Transcription with Timestamps and Labels

This format combines readable edited text, speaker labels, and timestamps. It can suit training sessions, presentations, interviews, and recorded discussions where navigation matters.

Include: readable text, accurate speaker turns, and timecodes in the agreed structure.

Do not: confuse editing with rewriting, remove caveats, or make a speaker's uncertain statement sound definite.

Specialized

10. Dialogue / Interview Transcription

Interview transcripts preserve the question-and-answer structure and clearly distinguish interviewer, interviewee, and any additional participant.

Include: each turn, relevant interruptions, overlapping speech where requested, and consistent speaker labels.

Do not: combine an answer with the question, rearrange the conversation for dramatic effect, or conceal a response that appears inconvenient.

Specialized

11. Academic / Lecture Transcription

Academic transcription captures explanations, definitions, examples, questions, and technical terminology. Mathematical and scientific material requires extra care because a small symbol or word can change the meaning.

Include: equations and symbols in a readable, conventional form where the source supports them; preserve spoken connectors such as “squared,” “divided by,” and “the square root of.”

Do not: guess an equation from context, silently correct a lecturer's calculation, or replace an unclear term with a plausible one. Mark genuine uncertainty according to the task rules.

Specialized

12. Meeting / Conference Transcription

Meeting transcripts capture contributions, decisions, questions, and action items in the form requested. Some projects require a full transcript; others specifically request edited meeting notes or a summary. These are different deliverables.

Include: speaker changes, names or roles when verified, decisions as actually stated, and relevant cross-talk if required.

Do not: call a summary a verbatim transcript, invent action owners, or record a proposal as an agreed decision unless the recording confirms it.

Specialized

13. Caption / Subtitle-Ready Transcription

This prepares spoken language for timed on-screen reading. The project may require line breaks, character limits, reading-speed rules, speaker identifiers, or non-speech sound labels.

Include: accurate speech, appropriate segmentation, and synchronization rules provided by the client.

Do not: assume a normal paragraph transcript is subtitle-ready, split phrases in confusing places, or remove meaning simply to fit a line without following the brief.

Specialized

14. Audio Description of Non-Speech Events

Some transcripts require relevant audible events such as laughter, applause, a door slam, or an extended pause. These descriptions should be neutral and consistent.

Include: meaningful events that the brief asks you to capture, using labels such as [laughter] or [applause].

Do not: over-describe ordinary background noise, infer emotions without evidence, or label a sound as a specific source when you cannot identify it reliably.

Specialized

15. Multilingual / Code-Switching Transcription

Speakers may move between languages or mix languages within a sentence. The transcript should represent the languages spoken according to the brief, including spelling and diacritics where possible.

Include: the original language, approved spelling, and language-change labels if required.

Do not: translate automatically when an original-language transcript was requested, replace unfamiliar words with guesses, or normalize dialect in a way that changes meaning.

04 · The working method

How to transcribe a recording properly

Quality comes from a repeatable process, not from typing quickly and hoping the audio agrees with you.

Read the task brief first

Identify the required transcription style, speaker-label convention, timestamp pattern, treatment of non-speech sounds, spelling preferences, file format, naming convention, and delivery instructions. Do not begin by assuming every project uses the same rules.

Check the source file

Confirm that the supplied audio or video opens and plays. Check that the recording appears complete and that the sound is usable. Report a damaged, missing, or clearly incomplete source rather than silently working around it.

Listen to understand the context

Listen to a passage before committing to difficult words. Context can help identify likely terms, but it is not permission to invent words that are not audible.

Draft the spoken content

Work in manageable sections. Capture words, speaker changes, figures, names, technical terms, and relevant vocal events. Follow the chosen verbatim or edited style from the beginning.

Replay difficult passages

Use playback controls to repeat unclear words and compare them with the surrounding sentence. Slow playback if useful, while remembering that excessive slowing can distort sounds. Do not replace uncertainty with a confident guess.

Add labels and timestamps

Apply the required speaker label and timecode format. Keep each timestamp attached to the passage it identifies. Check timecodes against the actual recording, especially after inserting or moving text.

Format the transcript

Apply the required header, company name, font, font size, spacing, paragraph layout, filename, and file type. Formatting is part of delivery, not decoration to be added if there is spare time.

Proofread while listening again

Follow the text while replaying the recording. Check omissions, repeated passages, speaker attribution, punctuation, numbers, names, mathematical notation, and timestamp placement.

Save and inspect the final DOCX

Open the saved document and verify that it is a real Word document, the content is complete, the layout is stable, and no text is clipped or missing. Use a clear filename and submit the requested file through the approved channel.

05 · Good practice

What to do, and what not to do

✓ Do this

  • Follow the exact task instructions and style.
  • Use repeated listening to resolve difficult speech.
  • Keep speaker labels stable and easy to distinguish.
  • Represent numbers, names, units, and technical terms carefully.
  • Use punctuation to clarify the speech without changing its meaning.
  • Mark unintelligible speech only when it genuinely cannot be recovered, using the required notation.
  • Keep the original meaning, uncertainty, and important qualifications.
  • Check the document against the source before submission.
  • Protect confidential recordings and files.
  • Raise source-quality problems or unclear instructions through the approved channel.

✕ Do not do this

  • Invent a word, speaker name, statistic, equation, or timestamp.
  • Use a guessed word merely because it makes the sentence sound better.
  • Change the speaker's opinion or correct factual claims without authorization.
  • Remove repetition or filler in a strict-verbatim assignment.
  • Keep every filler in a clean-verbatim assignment if the brief directs that non-essential clutter be removed.
  • Merge speakers or attach a statement to the wrong person.
  • Use inconsistent timecode formats or labels.
  • Submit a PDF, image, or plain-text file when DOCX is required.
  • Change font settings halfway through the document.
  • Copy another transcriber's work without authorization or expose source files publicly.
06 · Unclear speech

Handling difficult or unintelligible audio

Not every recording is clean. Noise, accents, overlapping speech, low volume, specialized vocabulary, and poor microphones can make words difficult to identify. A professional transcript distinguishes uncertainty from certainty.

Replay in context

Listen to the full sentence, then replay the difficult passage with a little surrounding audio. A short sound can be easier to recognize when heard as part of a phrase.

Use reference material carefully

Use approved glossaries, spellings, or supplied documents when available. A reference can verify a term, but it must not override what the recording actually says.

Mark genuine uncertainty

Use the project's prescribed label, such as [unintelligible] or [unclear], and include a timestamp if required. Do not use these labels as shortcuts for passages that simply need more listening.

Important: do not guess the speaker's identity, infer a word from a preferred answer, or silently omit a difficult phrase. If the task provides a specific uncertainty notation, use that notation consistently.

08 · Technical material

Mathematics, figures, and specialist terms

Educational and technical recordings need careful listening because a tiny change can alter the meaning of a whole explanation.

Mathematical transcription

  • Distinguish “squared” from “cubed,” “plus” from “minus,” and “less than” from “greater than.”
  • Capture fractions, exponents, subscripts, Greek letters, and equation relationships as clearly as the source permits.
  • Preserve spoken mathematical connectors such as “divided by,” “multiplied by,” “the square root of,” and “is equal to.”
  • Check decimal points, negative signs, percentages, units, and variable names.
  • Use conventional notation only when the spoken content supports it and the notation will not alter the meaning.

What to avoid

  • Do not silently fix an arithmetic mistake made by the speaker.
  • Do not replace a spoken expression with a different formula because it seems more mathematically correct.
  • Do not guess a symbol or number that is not audible.
  • Do not omit examples or explanations simply because they repeat an earlier point.
  • Do not turn an explanation into a summary unless the task specifically asks for a summary.

11 · Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Should every transcript include timestamps?

No. Include timestamps when the task requires them. A transcript can be speaker-labeled without timecodes, or timestamped without speaker labels. A combined format uses both.

Should grammar always be corrected?

No. Strict verbatim preserves speech details. Clean verbatim removes selected non-essential clutter. Intelligent transcription may improve readability, but it must preserve the speaker's meaning and must not invent or change claims.

What if two speakers talk at once?

Follow the task convention for overlapping speech. Keep each speaker distinct where possible. If words cannot be separated reliably, use the approved notation rather than assigning the words arbitrarily.

What if a technical word cannot be identified?

Replay it, check authorized reference material, and compare the sound with the context. If it remains unclear, mark it using the required uncertainty notation. Do not substitute a word just because it fits the topic.

Can the transcript be submitted as PDF?

This guide specifies DOCX for the covered PrimeTask Workspace submissions. Do not substitute PDF or another format unless the task instructions explicitly authorize it.

Can a transcriber summarize a long answer?

Not in a standard transcript. A summary is a different deliverable. Preserve the spoken content at the detail level required by the selected transcription style.

What takes priority if the task brief differs from this guide?

Follow the specific task instructions when they are clear and authorized. If two requirements conflict or an essential instruction is missing, ask the assigned reviewer or supervisor for clarification before submitting.

Accuracy is the standard. Clarity is the craft.

Every transcript should make the source easier to review without distorting what was said. Listen closely, follow the requested format, protect the source material, and verify the final document before submission.
